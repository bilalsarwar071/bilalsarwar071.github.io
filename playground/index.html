Interactive Formal Verification Lab

Jun 8, 2026 · 4 min read

I built these three tools to show how some of the ideas I work with actually behave. They are small, but they are real: the model checker explores every reachable state and checks your formula with the standard labelling algorithm, and the verifier computes weakest preconditions for whatever conditions you type in. Everything runs in your browser, so feel free to break things.

What you are looking at

DPLL SAT solver. A SAT solver decides whether a propositional formula has a satisfying assignment. DPLL guesses a value for one variable, follows every consequence it is forced into (unit propagation), and backtracks when a clause becomes false. The tree shows guesses as circles and forced assignments as diamonds. The pigeonhole example is worth stepping through: you cannot put three pigeons into two holes without two of them sharing, and the solver has to rule out every option before it can say so.

CTL model checker. The four systems are written as small guarded-command programs. The checker builds every reachable state from them, then works out which states satisfy each part of your formula, from the inside out. Press Step to watch that happen one subformula at a time. When a property fails you get a counterexample run, and for properties like EF you get a witness. Peterson’s algorithm passes everything. Remove the turn variable and it still keeps the processes apart, but it can deadlock. The naive lock fails at the most basic job, and the checker shows you the exact interleaving that breaks it.

Hoare logic verifier. A Hoare triple {P} S {Q} says that if P holds before program S runs, then Q holds after it. The verifier works backwards from Q, computing the weakest precondition of each statement, and turns the result into verification conditions (VCs). Loops need an invariant, which you provide. You can edit any of the conditions. Try weakening an invariant and see which VC breaks, and what counterexample it gives you.

Why this matters

Testing can show that a program works on the inputs you tried. Formal verification aims to show that it works on all of them, or to find the case where it does not. That is why these ideas show up wherever failures are expensive: hardware design, operating system kernels like seL4, flight software and communication protocols. Real tools are far more capable than these toys, but the core ideas are the same.
